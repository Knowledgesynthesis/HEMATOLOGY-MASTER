# A sleepy 6-week-old {peds #late-vkdb module=neonatal refs=aap-vitk-2022}
An exclusively breastfed infant born at home is vomiting and drowsy. Speed and the right products matter.

## Presentation
Ava, 6 weeks, was born at home. Her parents declined vitamin K. She is exclusively breastfed. Over 24 h she has become irritable, then lethargic, with three vomits. Exam: **bulging fontanelle**, oozing from a heel-stick site, mild scleral icterus.

:::check
What is the most appropriate immediate management?
- [ ] Head CT first, then decide on treatment
- [ ] IV vitamin K alone, and recheck the INR in 6 hours
- [x] IV vitamin K plus 4F-PCC (or plasma 10–15 mL/kg) now, with emergent neuroimaging and neurosurgery
- [ ] Platelet transfusion and IVIG
> Suspected late VKDB with possible ICH: vitamin K takes hours to work, so give PCC or plasma for immediate factor replacement. Imaging happens in parallel, not first.
:::

## Results
INR **> 10**, aPTT 98 s, platelets **310 × 10⁹/L**, fibrinogen **280 mg/dL**. CT: subdural and intraparenchymal hemorrhage.

:::check
Which feature best distinguishes VKDB from hemophilia A as the cause?
- [ ] Prolonged aPTT
- [x] Markedly prolonged PT/INR with normal platelets and fibrinogen
- [ ] Intracranial bleeding
- [ ] Female sex
> Hemophilia prolongs only the aPTT; the PT is normal. Vitamin K deficiency lowers factors II, VII, IX and X, so the PT/INR is strikingly prolonged. Normal platelets and fibrinogen argue against DIC.
:::

## Looking for the cause
Her INR corrects within 12 hours. Direct bilirubin is **2.4 mg/dL** and the stools are pale.

:::check
Beyond missed prophylaxis, what must be evaluated urgently?
- [ ] Hemophilia carrier testing in the mother
- [x] Cholestasis, especially biliary atresia (ultrasound, hepatology referral)
- [ ] Platelet function testing
- [ ] Factor XIII level
> Late VKDB often unmasks cholestatic liver disease, which impairs absorption of fat-soluble vitamin K. Biliary atresia outcomes depend on early surgery (Kasai), so this finding is time-critical.
:::

## Teaching points
- A **single IM vitamin K dose at birth** prevents almost all VKDB: 1 mg (> 1,500 g) or 0.3–0.5 mg/kg (≤ 1,500 g). Refusal raises the risk of late VKDB ~81-fold. About half of late VKDB presents with ICH.
- Oral regimens are less effective, especially in cholestasis.
- ICH in an infant normally prompts an abuse evaluation. A clear coagulopathy that explains the bleeding doesn't remove the need to document the history carefully.

# Chest pain on day two {peds #acs-case module=hemoglobinopathies refs=nhlbi-scd-2014,nacssg,bellet-1995,ash-scd-tx-2020}
A child admitted for a pain crisis becomes hypoxic. Recognize ACS early and escalate correctly.

## Admission
Malik, 11, HbSS, baseline Hb 8.2 g/dL, is admitted with rib and back pain. He is on a morphine PCA and IV fluids at **1.5× maintenance**. SpO₂ 97% on room air.

:::check
Which measure has the best evidence for preventing acute chest syndrome now?
- [ ] Supplemental oxygen to keep SpO₂ 100%
- [x] Incentive spirometry every 2 hours while awake
- [ ] Increase IV fluids to 2× maintenance
- [ ] Prophylactic dexamethasone
> Incentive spirometry reduced pulmonary complications in children with chest or back pain (Bellet 1995). Over-hydration can worsen lung injury, so keep fluids near maintenance. Oxygen without hypoxemia has no benefit.
:::

## Day 2
T 38.8 °C, RR 32, **SpO₂ 90%**, new **left lower lobe infiltrate**. Hb **6.8 g/dL**.

:::check
What is the best management?
- [ ] Ceftriaxone alone and continue fluids at 1.5× maintenance
- [x] Ceftriaxone plus azithromycin, oxygen, spirometry, fluids at maintenance, and simple transfusion
- [ ] Immediate exchange transfusion
- [ ] High-dose corticosteroids
> This is ACS with Hb > 1 g/dL below baseline. Cover typical and atypical organisms. Simple transfusion improves O₂ delivery. Exchange is reserved for severe or progressive disease.
:::

## Twelve hours later
SpO₂ **86% on 4 L**, bilateral infiltrates, increasing work of breathing. Hb is now **9.8 g/dL** after the transfusion.

:::check
What is the next step?
- [ ] Another simple transfusion to Hb 12 g/dL
- [x] Red cell exchange transfusion and ICU care
- [ ] Add furosemide and observe
- [ ] Switch antibiotics to meropenem only
> Progressive ACS needs exchange transfusion to lower HbS without raising viscosity. Another simple transfusion above ~10–11 g/dL risks hyperviscosity.
:::

## Before discharge
:::check
What is the most important long-term step?
- [ ] Nothing new; ACS is unpredictable
- [x] Start hydroxyurea (or confirm adherence and escalate to MTD); consider chronic transfusion if ACS recurs despite it
- [ ] Daily prophylactic azithromycin
- [ ] Splenectomy
> Hydroxyurea reduces ACS by about half. Recurrent ACS despite MTD is an indication for chronic transfusion or curative therapy discussion.
:::

# Dark urine after a mothball {peds #g6pd-case module=hemolysis refs=who-g6pd-2022}
Oxidant hemolysis in a toddler, and the test result that misleads.

## Presentation
Omar, 3, chewed a **naphthalene mothball** 2 days ago. He is pale and jaundiced with **cola-colored urine**. HR 150, BP normal. Hb **6.1 g/dL**, retic 6%, LDH 1,200 U/L, indirect bilirubin 4.2 mg/dL. Urinalysis: **blood 3+, 0–2 RBC/hpf**.

:::check
What does the urinalysis show?
- [ ] Hematuria from nephritis
- [x] Hemoglobinuria from intravascular hemolysis
- [ ] Myoglobinuria from rhabdomyolysis
- [ ] Contamination
> A dipstick strongly positive for heme with few or no RBCs on microscopy means free pigment. With anemia, high LDH and jaundice, that is hemoglobinuria.
:::

## Results
DAT negative. Smear: **bite and blister cells**. G6PD activity: **normal**.

:::check
How do you interpret the normal G6PD result?
- [ ] G6PD deficiency is excluded; look for PNH
- [x] Possibly falsely normal during reticulocytosis; repeat in 2–3 months (or interpret against the retic count, or test the mother)
- [ ] Diagnostic of an unstable hemoglobin
- [ ] Indicates naphthalene toxicity is not hemolytic
> The oldest, most deficient cells hemolyze first, and young reticulocytes have higher enzyme activity. A "normal" result during or soon after a crisis doesn't exclude deficiency.
:::

## Management
:::check
What is the best management now?
- [ ] Prednisone 2 mg/kg/day
- [x] Red cell transfusion, hydration, and removal of the trigger
- [ ] IVIG
- [ ] Splenectomy
> Acute G6PD hemolysis is self-limited once the oxidant is gone. Support the child (transfuse for symptomatic or severe anemia). Steroids and IVIG have no role.
:::

## Counseling
:::check
Which medication would be the most dangerous for him in the future?
- [ ] Amoxicillin
- [ ] Ondansetron
- [x] Rasburicase
- [ ] Cetirizine
> Rasburicase, primaquine/tafenoquine, dapsone and methylene blue can cause life-threatening hemolysis in G6PD deficiency. Fava beans and naphthalene too. Give the family a written list.
:::

# A jaundiced family {peds #hs-case module=hemolysis refs=bsh-hs-2011}
Hereditary spherocytosis from diagnosis to the splenectomy decision.

## Presentation
Grace, 5, has intermittent jaundice and a palpable spleen. Her father had his gallbladder and spleen removed in his twenties. Hb **9.6 g/dL**, MCHC **37.8 g/dL**, retic 9%, DAT negative, spherocytes on smear.

:::check
Which test confirms the diagnosis?
- [ ] Osmotic fragility on fresh blood
- [x] EMA binding by flow cytometry
- [ ] Hemoglobin electrophoresis
- [ ] Bone marrow aspirate
> EMA binding is the recommended confirmatory test. Family history, spherocytes, ↑MCHC and a negative DAT already make HS very likely.
:::

## Two weeks later
Her brother has bright red cheeks and a lacy rash. Grace is pale and tired: Hb **4.9 g/dL**, retic **0.3%**.

:::check
What is happening, and what do you do?
- [ ] Hemolytic crisis: steroids
- [x] Parvovirus B19 aplastic crisis: transfuse, droplet precautions, monitor until retic recovers
- [ ] Splenic sequestration: emergency splenectomy
- [ ] Iron deficiency: oral iron
> Parvovirus halts erythropoiesis for about a week. In chronic hemolysis, with its short RBC survival, Hb falls fast. The low retic is the key.
:::

## Age 7
She now needs ~2 transfusions a year, her growth is slowing, and ultrasound shows **gallstones** with episodes of biliary colic.

:::check
What is the best plan?
- [ ] Folic acid only
- [ ] Cholecystectomy alone
- [x] Splenectomy with cholecystectomy in the same procedure, after pneumococcal, meningococcal and Hib vaccination, then antibiotic prophylaxis and fever education
- [ ] Partial splenectomy only, because she is under 10
> Severe HS (transfusion needs, growth failure) is the indication for splenectomy. At ≥ 6 years total splenectomy is acceptable; partial splenectomy suits younger children. Remove symptomatic stones at the same time.
:::

## Teaching points
- HS is autosomal dominant in ~75%. Check siblings.
- After splenectomy: lifelong fever precautions, vaccines up to date, a small increase in thrombosis risk.

# Fever during induction {peds #fn-case module=marrow refs=fn-peds-2023}
Febrile neutropenia, then shock, then the persistent fever that changes the plan.

## Day 12 of ALL induction
Leila, 6, with a central line, has **T 38.7 °C** and **ANC 0.05 × 10⁹/L**. She looks well; BP and perfusion are normal.

:::check
What is the correct first step?
- [ ] Oral antibiotics and outpatient follow-up
- [x] Blood cultures from every CVC lumen and IV cefepime (or piperacillin-tazobactam) within 60 minutes
- [ ] Wait for the culture result
- [ ] Vancomycin plus meropenem plus amikacin by default
> Empiric antipseudomonal β-lactam monotherapy within 1 hour is the standard (2023 pediatric guideline). Add other agents only for instability or specific indications.
:::

## Three hours later
HR 170, capillary refill 4 s, BP 78/40, lactate 4 mmol/L.

:::check
What is the next step?
- [ ] Continue cefepime and recheck in the morning
- [x] Fluid resuscitation; broaden to cover resistant gram-negatives and gram-positives (e.g., meropenem + vancomycin ± aminoglycoside); PICU; consider stress-dose hydrocortisone after recent dexamethasone
- [ ] Remove the CVC immediately and stop antibiotics
- [ ] G-CSF and observation
> Septic shock in a neutropenic child is a different situation from stable febrile neutropenia. Recent high-dose steroids (induction) can cause adrenal suppression, so stress-dose hydrocortisone is often given for refractory shock.
:::

## Day 5 of fever
She is stable, but fever persists **≥ 96 h**. Cultures are negative and ANC is still 0.

:::check
What do you do next?
- [ ] Stop all antibiotics
- [x] Evaluate for invasive fungal infection (chest CT ± sinus/abdominal imaging) and start empiric antifungal therapy
- [ ] Repeat the same cultures and change nothing
- [ ] Give a granulocyte transfusion
> In high-risk patients, persistent fever beyond ~96 h of broad-spectrum antibiotics warrants a fungal work-up and empiric antifungal cover (e.g., caspofungin or liposomal amphotericin).
:::

# Abdominal mass and rising potassium {peds #burkitt-case module=lymphoma refs=inter-b-nhl}
The fastest-growing human tumor, and its metabolic emergency.

## Presentation
Sami, 8, has 10 days of abdominal pain and distension. Ultrasound: a large **ileocecal mass** with ascites. LDH **2,800 U/L**, uric acid **11.4 mg/dL**, K **5.6**, phosphate **7.2 mg/dL**, Ca 8.0 mg/dL, creatinine **1.1 mg/dL** (baseline 0.4).

:::check
What is the most urgent priority?
- [ ] Laparotomy for tissue diagnosis
- [x] Treat tumor lysis: hyperhydration without potassium and rasburicase (after G6PD check), with close electrolyte monitoring
- [ ] Urine alkalinization with bicarbonate
- [ ] Start chemotherapy before any supportive measures
> He already meets laboratory TLS criteria (uric acid ≥ 8, phosphate ≥ 6.5 in children, rising creatinine). Rasburicase lowers uric acid within hours. Alkalinization is no longer recommended.
:::

## Diagnosis
:::check
What is the least invasive way to make the diagnosis?
- [ ] Excisional laparotomy
- [x] Ascitic fluid (or marrow) cytology with flow cytometry and MYC studies
- [ ] PET-CT only
- [ ] Empiric steroids, then reassess
> Burkitt cells are often present in effusions or marrow. A paracentesis can give the diagnosis without the risks of major surgery in an unstable child.
:::

## Day 2 of chemotherapy
K **6.9** with peaked T waves, phosphate **11 mg/dL**, urine output falling.

:::check
What is the definitive next step?
- [ ] Oral potassium binder only
- [x] Emergency treatment of hyperkalemia (calcium, insulin/glucose, β-agonist) and urgent hemodialysis
- [ ] Furosemide alone
- [ ] Stop chemotherapy permanently
> Refractory hyperkalemia, severe hyperphosphatemia and oliguria in TLS are indications for dialysis. Have nephrology involved before chemotherapy starts.
:::

## Teaching points
With intensive chemotherapy plus rituximab for high-risk disease (Inter-B-NHL ritux 2010), cure rates exceed 90%.

# Can't lie flat {peds #mediastinal-case module=leukemia refs=who-haem5-lymphoid}
The anterior mediastinal mass: diagnose it without killing the patient.

## Presentation
Jonah, 14, has 2 weeks of cough and now can't sleep lying down. His face is puffy and his neck veins are distended. CXR: large **anterior mediastinal mass** and right pleural effusion. WBC 22 × 10⁹/L with atypical mononuclear cells.

:::check
What is the safest route to diagnosis?
- [ ] CT-guided mass biopsy under general anesthesia
- [x] Peripheral blood flow cytometry, and/or thoracentesis under local anesthesia, sitting upright
- [ ] Mediastinoscopy
- [ ] Start steroids, then biopsy when he is better
> Sedation or general anesthesia in the supine position can cause airway or cardiovascular collapse. Circulating abnormal cells and a pleural effusion offer a diagnosis without anesthesia.
:::

## Results
Flow cytometry: **T-lymphoblasts** (cytoplasmic CD3+, TdT+, CD1a+).

:::check
Before any procedure needing sedation (lumbar puncture, central line), what is essential?
- [ ] Nothing special
- [x] An airway and cardiovascular risk assessment (CT tracheal compression, echocardiogram) with anesthesia planning; often defer sedation until steroids shrink the mass
- [ ] Platelet transfusion
- [ ] Prophylactic intubation in the ward
> Tracheal compression > 50%, pericardial effusion or SVC obstruction are high-risk features. Teams often start steroids first and perform the LP once the airway is safer.
:::

## Teaching points
- T-ALL is commonest in **adolescent boys**: high WBC, mediastinal mass, CNS involvement.
- **TLS risk is high.** Start hydration and uric-acid control before steroids.
- Nelarabine is part of modern frontline therapy.

# An 8-month-old who stopped growing {peds #thal-major-case module=hemoglobinopathies refs=tif-2021,casgevy-peds-2026}
Transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia, from first transfusion to cure.

## Presentation
Eleni, 8 months, has poor feeding, pallor, poor weight gain and an enlarging abdomen. Hb **5.4 g/dL**, MCV **58 fL**, retic 2%, many nucleated RBCs and targets, liver and spleen palpable. Her newborn screen read "**F only**".

:::check
What is the most likely diagnosis?
- [ ] Iron deficiency anemia
- [ ] HbH disease
- [x] β-thalassemia major (β⁰/β⁰), presenting as HbF falls
- [ ] Diamond-Blackfan anemia
> No HbA on the newborn screen, severe microcytic anemia at 6–24 months, and extramedullary hematopoiesis fit β⁰-thalassemia major. Confirm with HPLC and DNA studies.
:::

## Management
:::check
What is the right transfusion strategy?
- [ ] Transfuse only when Hb falls below 5 g/dL
- [x] Regular transfusions every 3–4 weeks, keeping pre-transfusion Hb 9.5–10.5 g/dL, with leukoreduced, extended phenotype-matched red cells
- [ ] One transfusion, then folic acid
- [ ] Hydroxyurea instead of transfusion
> TIF targets suppress ineffective erythropoiesis, preventing bone deformity, hepatosplenomegaly and growth failure. Phenotype matching reduces alloimmunization.
:::

## Age 3
After ~25 transfusions, ferritin is **1,450 ng/mL**.

:::check
What should start now?
- [ ] Phlebotomy
- [x] Iron chelation (e.g., deferasirox), with liver iron MRI and cardiac T2* monitoring as she grows
- [ ] Stop transfusions for 6 months
- [ ] Vitamin C supplements
> Start chelation after ~10–20 transfusions or ferritin > 1,000 ng/mL. Cardiac iron is the leading cause of death in TDT.
:::

## Cure
Her parents ask about curative options. She has an HLA-matched brother without thalassemia.

:::check
Which statement is most accurate?
- [ ] No curative options exist for children
- [x] Matched sibling HSCT in childhood offers > 90% survival; gene therapy (including exa-cel, US-approved from age 2) is an alternative in specialized centers
- [ ] Mitapivat is the first-line cure for children
- [ ] Luspatercept is curative
> Matched sibling HSCT has the longest track record. Mitapivat and luspatercept are disease-modifying and approved for adults only.
:::

Evidence-based transfusion thresholds for a 25-week infant.

Using TOP restrictive thresholds, what should you do?

- [ ] Give erythropoietin instead
- [ ] Transfuse to Hb 15 g/dL

What is the best initial approach?

- [x] Recognize TAM: observe with serial CBCs, test for GATA1, and manage polycythemia and feeding supportively

Asparaginase-associated cerebral sinovenous thrombosis.

## Presentation
Sofia, 15, is in ALL consolidation and recently received pegaspargase. She has 3 days of worsening headache and vomiting, papilledema, and a seizure this morning.

:::check
What is the most likely diagnosis, and what is the next test?

- [x] Cerebral sinovenous thrombosis: MRI with MR venography

- [ ] Aspirin only
