## A three-question approach to anemia {both #approach refs=harriet-lane,who-hb-2024}

:::algo
1. **Is it real, and how sick is the patient?** Use age- and sex-specific ranges. Check vitals, whether it is acute or chronic, and whether other cell lines are involved (if so, think marrow: see [red flags](/tools/leukemia-red-flags)).
2. **Production or loss/destruction?** Absolute retic or RPI: low = under-production; high = hemolysis or bleeding.
3. **What size are the cells?** Age-adjusted MCV: micro / normo / macro.
4. **Then the smear.** It often gives the answer (spherocytes, schistocytes, sickle cells, targets, blasts, hypersegmented neutrophils).
:::

**Most likely causes by age (pediatrics)**

| Age | Think first |
|---|---|
| Newborn | Blood loss, alloimmune hemolysis, hereditary hemolytic disease, infection |
| 2–3 months | Physiologic nadir (normal); preterm anemia |
| 6–36 months | **Iron deficiency** (milk), lead, TEC, thalassemia trait, SCD presenting |
| School age | Chronic disease, hemoglobinopathy, HS, ALL (if other lines are low) |
| Adolescent | **Menstrual iron deficiency**, heavy menstrual bleeding with VWD, IBD/celiac, eating disorders, B12 (diet, nitrous oxide) |

**Adults:** iron deficiency (GI loss, menses), anemia of inflammation and CKD, B12/folate, MDS (if older with macrocytosis or other cytopenias), myeloma (if rouleaux, renal failure, hypercalcemia).

:::check

- [ ] Iron deficiency anemia
- [x] Transient erythroblastopenia of childhood
- [ ] Diamond-Blackfan anemia
- [ ] Hereditary spherocytosis in aplastic crisis
> A normocytic anemia with retic near zero, in a previously well 1–4-year-old with other lines normal, is classic TEC. It recovers spontaneously within 1–2 months. DBA presents earlier (< 1 year), is often macrocytic with ↑HbF and ↑eADA, and may have physical anomalies. See the [Marrow module](/learn/marrow#inherited-bmf).
:::

## Iron deficiency in infants, children and adolescents (AAP 2026) {peds #ida-peds refs=aap-iron-2026,who-ferritin-2020,powers-2017,aap-iron-2010}

:::update AAP replaced its 2010 guidance {year=2026}
The 2026 AAP clinical report (with ASPHO) moves to **risk-based screening with CBC plus ferritin** and **universal screening of menstruating adolescents**. It reaffirms **once-daily oral iron** as first-line treatment [@aap-iron-2026].
:::

**Who is at risk**
- Preterm birth.
- Exclusively breastfed beyond ~6 months without iron or iron-rich foods.
- **Cow's or plant-based milk before 12 months, or > 24 oz/day after.**
- Low dietary iron or food insecurity; strict vegetarian/vegan diet; obesity.
- GI blood loss or epistaxis; IBD, celiac, CKD, rheumatologic disease.
- Menstruating adolescents, especially with heavy menstrual bleeding; endurance athletes; blood donors.

**Why milk causes it:** milk is low in bioavailable iron, displaces iron-rich foods, and calcium/casein inhibit absorption. In infants < 12 months, cow's milk protein can also cause occult GI bleeding.

**Screening (AAP 2026 as reported)**
- **Breastfed infants:** ~9–12 months.
- **Formula-fed infants:** ~15–18 months, after switching to cow's or plant milk.
- **At-risk children:** yearly to age 4.
- **All menstruating adolescents:** ≥ 1 year after menarche or by age 14; **yearly if heavy menstrual bleeding.**

**Diagnosis:** CBC plus ferritin. Ferritin defines iron deficiency at **< 20 ng/mL (young and school-age children)** and **< 30 ng/mL (adolescents)** [@aap-iron-2026]. Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant; with infection or inflammation, WHO uses < 30 (under 5 years) and < 70 µg/L (5 years and older) [@who-ferritin-2020].

**Prevention**
- Delayed cord clamping.
- **Preterm:** 2–3 mg/kg/day elemental iron from the first weeks through 12 months.
- **Exclusively breastfed term infants:** 1 mg/kg/day elemental iron by 4–6 months, until iron-rich foods are established.
- **No cow's milk before 12 months**; **≤ 16–24 oz/day** after.

**Treatment**
- **3 mg/kg/day elemental iron, once daily** (ferrous sulfate), on an empty stomach, **not with milk** [@powers-2017].
- **Adolescents: 65 mg elemental iron daily** (one 325-mg ferrous sulfate tablet).
- Fix the cause at the same time (milk volume, menstrual control, GI evaluation).
- **Follow up at ~1 and 3 months.** Retic rises by 5–10 days; expect **Hb ↑ ≥ 1–2 g/dL by 4 weeks.**
- **Treat ~3 months total**, longer if ferritin remains < 20.
- **IV iron** for oral failure despite adherence, malabsorption, or ongoing loss. Ferric carboxymaltose is approved from age 1 (watch for hypophosphatemia).

:::pitfall Severe but stable
Toddlers with chronic IDA tolerate Hb 4–6 g/dL remarkably well. If the child is **hemodynamically stable, oral iron with close follow-up** is appropriate. If transfusion is needed, give **small aliquots slowly** (e.g., 5 mL/kg over 3–4 h). Rapid volume can precipitate heart failure.
:::

**When oral iron "fails":**
- Non-adherence or wrong administration (given with milk).
- Ongoing loss.
- Celiac, *H. pylori*, IBD.
- Wrong diagnosis: thalassemia trait, lead, inflammation.
- **IRIDA** (TMPRSS6).

:::pearl
Iron deficiency in infancy is linked to **lasting neurocognitive effects even without anemia**. That is why prevention and screening matter more here than at any other age.
:::

:::check
An 18-month-old drinks 40 oz of cow's milk a day. Hb 6.8 g/dL, MCV 58 fL, ferritin 3 ng/mL. He is alert with normal vitals. What is the best management?
- [ ] Admit for 15 mL/kg PRBC transfusion
- [x] Oral elemental iron 3 mg/kg once daily, limit milk to ≤ 16–24 oz/day, recheck in ~4 weeks

:::

## Iron deficiency in adults {adult #ida-adult refs=aga-ida-2020,stoffel-2017}

- **Diagnosis:** ferritin < 30 ng/mL (high specificity). **< 45 ng/mL triggers GI evaluation** (AGA). With inflammation, ferritin up to ~100 with **TSAT < 20%** suggests iron deficiency.
- **Find the source.** **Men and postmenopausal women:** bidirectional endoscopy. Non-invasive celiac and *H. pylori* testing [@aga-ida-2020]. **Premenopausal women:** menstrual history first; GI work-up if symptoms, age, family history, or refractory.
- **Oral iron:** 40–65 mg elemental **once daily or on alternate days**. Hepcidin rises for ~24 h after a dose, so alternate-day dosing increases fractional absorption and tolerability [@stoffel-2017]. Expect Hb ↑ ~1–2 g/dL in 3–4 weeks.
- **IV iron indications:** intolerance or failure, malabsorption (bariatric, IBD, celiac), ongoing loss, CKD, 2nd/3rd-trimester pregnancy, heart failure with iron deficiency, short preoperative window. Ferric carboxymaltose can cause **symptomatic hypophosphatemia**; ferric derisomaltose has lower risk.
- **IV dosing (Ganzoni):** total deficit (mg) = weight (kg) × (target − actual Hb, g/dL) × 2.4 + stores (500 mg). See the [calculator](/tools/calculators).

## Anemia of inflammation and CKD {both #inflammation refs=who-ferritin-2020}

- **Mechanism:** IL-6 → ↑hepcidin → iron sequestration. Also blunted EPO response and shorter RBC survival.
- **Labs:** normocytic (sometimes mildly microcytic), low retic, **↓TSAT with normal/↑ferritin**, low-normal TIBC, **normal sTfR**.
- **Combined iron deficiency + inflammation** is common (IBD, CKD, heart failure, obesity): ferritin 30–100, TSAT < 20%, ↑sTfR (sTfR/log ferritin > 2). These patients usually need IV iron.
- **Pediatric settings:** IBD, JIA, chronic infection, CKD, obesity (↑hepcidin).
- **CKD:** EPO deficiency plus iron restriction. Replete iron first, then ESA. Avoid targeting normal Hb in adults (higher stroke and CV risk).

## Lead poisoning {peds #lead refs=cdc-lead-2021}

- **CDC blood lead reference value: 3.5 µg/dL** (lowered from 5 in 2021) [@cdc-lead-2021]. **There is no safe level.**
- **Screen:** at **12 and 24 months** for Medicaid-enrolled and high-risk children (old housing, recent immigration, pica, contaminated spices/cosmetics/ceramics, imported products). Confirm capillary results by venous sample.
- **Hematologic effects:** lead inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, causing ↑zinc protoporphyrin and **basophilic stippling**. Anemia usually appears only at high levels. **Microcytosis in a lead-exposed child is usually concurrent iron deficiency**, which also **increases lead absorption**.

| Venous BLL (µg/dL) | Action |
|---|---|
| ≥ 3.5 | Confirm, exposure history, iron/calcium nutrition, development monitoring, repeat testing |
| ≥ 20 | Environmental investigation; abdominal X-ray if ingestion suspected |
| **≥ 45** | **Chelation** (oral succimer) once the child is in a lead-safe home |
| ≥ 70 or encephalopathy | **Admit**: IV CaNa₂EDTA ± dimercaprol; medical emergency |

## Thalassemia trait vs iron deficiency {both #thal-vs-ida refs=mentzer,tif-2021}

| Feature | Thalassemia trait | Iron deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Hb | Mildly low or normal | Low (proportional to MCV) |
| MCV | **Very low for the Hb** (60s) | Low |
| RBC count | **Normal or high** (> 5.0 × 10¹²/L) | Low |
| **Mentzer index** (MCV ÷ RBC) | **< 13** | **> 13** |
| RDW | Normal or mildly ↑ | ↑ |
| Ferritin | Normal | Low |
| HPLC/electrophoresis | **β-trait: HbA₂ > 3.5%**; α-trait: normal after infancy | Normal (HbA₂ may be low) |

- Mentzer is a screening clue only. It is **less accurate in young children** and in mixed states. Confirm with ferritin and HPLC.
- **Iron deficiency lowers HbA₂ and can mask β-thal trait.** Repeat HPLC after iron repletion.
- **α-thal trait** has a normal adult HPLC. Infer it (normal ferritin, normal HbA₂, ancestry, Hb Barts on the newborn screen) or confirm by **α-globin gene testing**. This matters for **genetic counseling**: a **cis (--/αα) carrier** partnered with another can have a fetus with Hb Barts hydrops.
- **Don't give repeated empiric iron to a child with persistent microcytosis.** Measure ferritin, then do HPLC.

:::check
A 25-year-old has Hb 11.6 g/dL, MCV 63 fL, RBC 5.9 × 10¹²/L, RDW 14%, ferritin 85 ng/mL, HbA₂ 5.1%. What is the diagnosis?
- [ ] Iron deficiency
- [x] β-thalassemia trait
- [ ] α-thalassemia trait
- [ ] Anemia of inflammation
> Mentzer 63/5.9 ≈ 10.7 (< 13), high RBC count, normal ferritin and HbA₂ > 3.5% confirm β-thal trait. No treatment is needed. Offer partner testing and genetic counseling.
:::

## Sideroblastic anemias {both #sideroblastic refs=who-haem5-myeloid}

- **Hallmark:** **ring sideroblasts** (iron-laden mitochondria around the nucleus) on marrow Prussian blue stain; Pappenheimer bodies on the smear. Iron studies show **↑ferritin and ↑TSAT**, with a risk of overload.
- **Congenital** [[peds]]:
  - **X-linked ALAS2** (microcytic; **often pyridoxine-responsive**).
  - SLC25A38 (severe, recessive).
  - **Pearson syndrome** (mtDNA deletion): infantile sideroblastic anemia, **vacuolated marrow precursors**, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, lactic acidosis.
- **Acquired** [[adult]]:
  - **MDS with SF3B1 mutation** (often normocytic/macrocytic, dimorphic).
  - Alcohol, **isoniazid** (give pyridoxine), linezolid.
  - **Copper deficiency** (zinc excess from supplements or denture cream, bariatric surgery). It also causes neutropenia and a **B12-like myeloneuropathy**.

## Macrocytic anemias, B12 and folate {both #macrocytic refs=nathan-oski}

**Megaloblastic (impaired DNA synthesis):** B12, folate, drugs (methotrexate, hydroxyurea, azathioprine/6-MP, zidovudine), rare inborn errors (TCN2 deficiency, cblC, Imerslund-Gräsbeck, thiamine-responsive anemia, orotic aciduria). Marrow can be hypercellular with ineffective erythropoiesis (**↑LDH, ↑indirect bilirubin**, low retic) and **hypersegmented neutrophils**. Pancytopenia is possible.

**Non-megaloblastic:** reticulocytosis, liver disease, alcohol, hypothyroidism, MDS, aplastic anemia, **DBA and Fanconi anemia** (macrocytosis may precede cytopenias), valproate. Macrocytosis is **normal in neonates** and common in Down syndrome.

**Pediatric B12 deficiency** [[peds]]
- **Exclusively breastfed infants of B12-deficient mothers** (vegan diet, pernicious anemia, bariatric surgery) present at 4–12 months with **FTT, hypotonia, developmental regression**, tremor and megaloblastic anemia. Treat the infant with IM B12 **and evaluate the mother**.
- **Adolescents using nitrous oxide** ("whippets") inactivate B12. They present with **myeloneuropathy even with a normal serum B12**. Check MMA and homocysteine.
- **Goat's milk** is deficient in folate and B12.

**Labs:** B12 < 200 pg/mL is low; 200–300 is borderline → **MMA (↑ in B12 deficiency)** and **homocysteine (↑ in both B12 and folate deficiency)**. MMA rises falsely in renal failure. In pernicious anemia, **anti-intrinsic factor antibody is specific** and anti-parietal antibody is sensitive.

**Treatment**
- **B12:** IM cyanocobalamin 1,000 µg daily ×1 week → weekly ×4 → monthly. **High-dose oral 1,000–2,000 µg/day works even in pernicious anemia** (passive absorption) once neurologic disease is not severe. In infants, IM dosing is weight-adjusted.
- **Folate:** 1 mg/day.
- **Never give folate alone when B12 deficiency is possible.** It can correct the anemia while neurologic damage progresses.
- **Watch potassium** in the first days of treating severe megaloblastic anemia.
