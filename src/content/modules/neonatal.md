## Neonatal anemia and the physiologic nadir {peds #neonatal-anemia refs=nathan-oski,penut,bsh-neonatal-2016}

**Physiologic nadir**
- **Term infants:** nadir at **8–12 weeks**, Hb ~9.5–11 g/dL. It needs no treatment.
- **Preterm infants (anemia of prematurity):** nadir is earlier (4–8 weeks) and deeper (7–9 g/dL), from lower EPO, shorter RBC survival, rapid growth and phlebotomy losses.

**Pathologic anemia at birth**

| Mechanism | Clues | Tests |
|---|---|---|
| Blood loss: fetomaternal, twin–twin, placental, internal (subgaleal, liver/spleen) | Pallor without jaundice. Acute: normal Hb at first, then shock. Chronic: low MCV, well compensated | **Kleihauer-Betke or flow cytometry** for HbF cells in **maternal** blood; head US |
| Hemolysis: alloimmune, HS, G6PD, PK, α-thal | **Jaundice < 24 h**, high retic, smear changes | DAT, smear, bilirubin, G6PD |
| Under-production: parvovirus, congenital infection, DBA | **Low retic** | Parvovirus PCR; DBA (macrocytosis, ↑eADA) |

**Prevention:** delayed cord clamping (≥ 30–60 s in vigorous term and preterm infants) raises Hb and iron stores. Minimize phlebotomy (micro-sampling, point-of-care testing).

:::pearl
Erythropoietin modestly reduces transfusions in preterm infants. High-dose EPO **did not provide neuroprotection** in PENUT [@penut]. It is not routine everywhere.
:::

:::check
A term newborn is pale and tachycardic at 1 hour of life, not jaundiced, with Hb 9.8 g/dL and normal DAT. Which test on the **mother** is most useful?
- [ ] Indirect antiglobulin (antibody screen)
- [x] Kleihauer-Betke or flow cytometry for fetal cells
- [ ] Hemoglobin electrophoresis

> Pallor without jaundice at birth suggests blood loss. Fetomaternal hemorrhage is diagnosed by quantifying fetal (HbF) cells in maternal circulation. Acute large hemorrhage may need emergency O-negative RBCs.
:::

## Preterm transfusion thresholds {peds #preterm-thresholds refs=top,ettno,planet-2,aabb-plt-2025}

Two large trials (TOP, ETTNO; together > 2,700 infants < 1,000 g) found **higher Hb thresholds did not reduce death or neurodevelopmental impairment** [@top] [@ettno]. Use restrictive thresholds.

**TOP restrictive thresholds (Hb g/dL)**

| Postnatal week | With respiratory support | Without |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | 11.0 | 10.0 |
| Week 2 | 10.0 | 8.5 |
| Week ≥ 3 | 8.5 | 7.0 |

**Platelets:** PlaNeT-2 (< 34 weeks) found that transfusing below **50** rather than **25 × 10⁹/L** *increased* death or major bleeding (26% vs 19%) [@planet-2]. AABB/ICTMG 2025 suggests a **25 × 10⁹/L** threshold for non-bleeding preterm neonates [@aabb-plt-2025].

**Plasma:** no benefit for prophylaxis, volume expansion, or "correcting" a prolonged PT/aPTT in a non-bleeding neonate.

:::check
A 27-week infant on day 10 has platelets 32 × 10⁹/L, no bleeding, and is stable on CPAP. What is the best action?
- [ ] Transfuse platelets 15 mL/kg now
- [x] No platelet transfusion; monitor
- [ ] Give IVIG 1 g/kg
- [ ] Transfuse plasma 10 mL/kg
> The count is above the evidence-based 25 × 10⁹/L threshold. PlaNeT-2 showed prophylactic transfusion at higher thresholds causes net harm.
:::

## Hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) {peds #hdfn refs=aap-bili-2022,bsh-neonatal-2016}

| | ABO HDFN | Rh(D) HDFN | Anti-Kell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setting | Mother **O**, baby A or B | D-negative mother **sensitized** earlier | Any prior sensitization (often transfusion) |
| First pregnancy? | **Yes** (natural IgG anti-A/B) | Rarely | Yes, if sensitized |
| Severity | Usually mild: early jaundice | Can cause **hydrops**, severe anemia | Anemia **out of proportion to bilirubin** |
| DAT | **Often weak or negative** | Strongly positive | Positive |
| Smear | **Spherocytes**, ↑retic | Retic ↑, nRBCs | **Low retic** (suppresses erythroid precursors) |

**Prevention:** RhIG to D-negative mothers at ~28 weeks, within 72 h of delivering a D-positive infant, and after sensitizing events (bleeding, trauma, procedures). **No prophylaxis exists for ABO or Kell.**

**Antenatal:** antibody titers → fetal MCA peak systolic velocity > 1.5 MoM → intrauterine transfusion.

**Postnatal management**
1. Cord blood type and DAT, Hb, bilirubin early. Hour-specific bilirubin monitoring.
2. Intensive phototherapy per [AAP 2022 thresholds](#hyperbilirubinemia). Isoimmune hemolysis is a **neurotoxicity risk factor**, which lowers the threshold.
3. **IVIG 0.5–1 g/kg** if TSB keeps rising despite intensive phototherapy or is within 2 mg/dL of the exchange threshold [@aap-bili-2022].
4. Exchange transfusion for TSB at the exchange threshold or signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy.
5. **Follow Hb weekly for 2–3 months.** Late hyporegenerative anemia (weeks 2–12) is common, especially after IUT or IVIG.

:::pitfall
A negative DAT does **not** exclude ABO HDFN. The antigen sites are weakly developed on neonatal RBCs. Spherocytes, high retic, and early jaundice in an O-mother/A-baby pair are enough.
:::

## Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia: AAP 2022 essentials {peds #hyperbilirubinemia refs=aap-bili-2022}

- **Every newborn gets a TSB or TcB at 24–48 h or before discharge.** Visual estimation is unreliable.
- **Jaundice in the first 24 hours is pathologic.** Measure TSB immediately and evaluate for hemolysis.
- Thresholds are **hour-specific curves by gestational age**. They were raised in 2022, which reduces over-treatment. Use the AAP curves or BiliTool.
- **Neurotoxicity risk factors** (use the lower curves): GA < 38 weeks, albumin < 3.0 g/dL, **isoimmune hemolysis, G6PD deficiency or other hemolysis**, sepsis, clinical instability in the past 24 h.
- **Escalation of care:** TSB within 2 mg/dL of exchange threshold → urgent intensive phototherapy, labs (CBC, type/DAT, albumin, type and crossmatch), and consider IVIG if isoimmune.
- **Test G6PD activity** in infants on phototherapy whose family history or ancestry suggests deficiency, or who respond poorly to phototherapy.
- **Direct (conjugated) bilirubin > 1.0 mg/dL is abnormal at any age.** Evaluate for cholestasis (biliary atresia: pale stools; urgent).

:::redflag Acute bilirubin encephalopathy
Lethargy, hypotonia, poor feeding, then a high-pitched cry, hypertonia and retrocollis/opisthotonos. Do an **immediate exchange transfusion** regardless of the TSB value.
:::

## Neonatal polycythemia {peds #polycythemia refs=nathan-oski}

- **Definition:** **venous** Hct > 65%. Capillary values run higher, so always confirm with a venous sample. Hct peaks ~2 h after birth.
- **Causes:**
  - Placental insufficiency (SGA, preeclampsia, smoking, post-term).
  - **Infant of a diabetic mother.**
  - Trisomy 21/13/18, Beckwith-Wiedemann.
  - Twin–twin recipient.
  - Delayed clamping with excessive placental transfusion.
- **Effects of hyperviscosity:** plethora, lethargy, poor feeding, respiratory distress, **hypoglycemia**, hyperbilirubinemia, thrombocytopenia, NEC, renal vein thrombosis.
- **Management:** hydration and glucose. **Partial exchange transfusion** with normal saline is reserved for symptomatic infants (often Hct > 65%) or very high asymptomatic values (> 70–75%). Trials show **no long-term neurodevelopmental benefit** and some increase in NEC.
- **Partial exchange volume** = blood volume (80–90 mL/kg × kg) × (observed Hct − target Hct) / observed Hct. The target is usually 55%. Use the [calculator](/tools/calculators).

## Neonatal thrombocytopenia and FNAIT {peds #neonatal-thrombocytopenia refs=planet-2,bsh-neonatal-2016,aabb-plt-2025}

**Sort by timing and whether the baby is sick.**

| | Well-appearing | Sick |
|---|---|---|
| **Early (< 72 h)** | **Placental insufficiency/SGA** (most common; mild–moderate, resolves in 7–10 days). **FNAIT.** Maternal ITP/SLE. Aneuploidy (T13/18/21). Inherited (CAMT, TAR, WAS, MYH9) | Perinatal asphyxia, early-onset sepsis, congenital CMV/TORCH, DIC, thrombosis |
| **Late (> 72 h)** | Drug (heparin), inherited | **Late-onset sepsis, NEC** (most common), fungal infection, catheter thrombosis |

**FNAIT (fetal/neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia).** The neonatal counterpart of Rh disease, but for platelets.
- Maternal IgG against a **paternal platelet antigen** (HPA-1a in ~75–80% of cases in white populations; HPA-5b, HPA-15, HPA-4 in Asian populations).
- **Occurs in first pregnancies.** Picture: a **well term baby with petechiae and severe thrombocytopenia (< 30–50 × 10⁹/L)**.
- **ICH** affects up to 10–20% of severe cases, often **in utero**. Get a **cranial ultrasound in every case.**
- **Treat:** platelets if < 30 × 10⁹/L (higher with ICH). Use HPA-selected platelets if immediately available, but **don't delay random-donor platelets** while waiting. Add IVIG 1 g/kg.
- **Confirm:** maternal anti-HPA antibodies plus parental HPA genotyping.
- **Next pregnancy:** recurrence is common and often more severe. Antenatal maternal IVIG ± prednisone from the 2nd trimester.

**Neonate of a mother with ITP:** usually mild. The count nadirs on **days 2–5**, so check daily for 3–5 days. Give IVIG for < 30 × 10⁹/L or bleeding. ICH is rare.

**Clues to inherited thrombocytopenia:**
- **Absent radii with thumbs present** → TAR.
- **Small platelets + eczema + boy** → Wiskott-Aldrich.
- **Giant platelets** → MYH9 (look for Döhle-like inclusions) or Bernard-Soulier.
- **Severe thrombocytopenia, normal exam, no maternal antibody** → CAMT (MPL).

:::check
A well, term 1-day-old has scattered petechiae and platelets 12 × 10⁹/L. Mother's count is normal; this is her first pregnancy. What is the most likely diagnosis?
- [ ] Neonatal effects of maternal ITP
- [x] Fetal/neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia
- [ ] Placental insufficiency thrombocytopenia
- [ ] Congenital CMV
> Severe isolated thrombocytopenia in a well term infant with a mother whose count is normal is FNAIT until proven otherwise, and it happens in first pregnancies. Get a head ultrasound, transfuse platelets (don't wait for antigen-negative units), give IVIG, and send maternal/paternal HPA studies.
:::

## Vitamin K deficiency bleeding (VKDB) {peds #vkdb refs=aap-vitk-2022}

| Form | Timing | Typical setting | Bleeding sites |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | < 24 h | Maternal anticonvulsants, rifampin, isoniazid, warfarin | Cephalohematoma, ICH, GI |
| Classic | Days 2–7 | No prophylaxis, breastfed | GI, umbilical, **circumcision**, puncture sites |
| **Late** | **2–12 weeks** (to 6 months) | **Exclusively breastfed and no IM vitamin K**, or **cholestasis/malabsorption** (biliary atresia, CF, α1-antitrypsin deficiency) | **ICH in ~50%**, high mortality and morbidity |

- **Labs:** **markedly prolonged PT/INR**, prolonged aPTT, **normal platelets and fibrinogen**. ↑PIVKA-II confirms.
- **Prevention:** **single IM vitamin K at birth**: 1 mg (> 1,500 g) or 0.3–0.5 mg/kg (≤ 1,500 g) [@aap-vitk-2022]. Oral regimens are less effective, particularly against late VKDB, and there is no FDA-approved oral product. **Infants who don't receive vitamin K have ~81× the risk of late VKDB.**
- **Treatment:** vitamin K 1 mg IV (slowly) or SC. For serious bleeding add **4F-PCC or plasma 10–15 mL/kg** — vitamin K takes hours to work.

:::redflag
A 6-week-old exclusively breastfed infant whose parents declined vitamin K presents with vomiting, irritability, a bulging fontanelle and a very high INR. That is **late VKDB with ICH**. Give vitamin K **and** PCC/plasma immediately, get neuroimaging, and look for cholestasis (fractionated bilirubin).
:::

:::check
A 5-week-old with jaundice and pale stools has a bruise and INR 4.5. Platelets and fibrinogen are normal. What is the underlying mechanism?
- [ ] Hemophilia A
- [x] Vitamin K malabsorption from cholestasis
- [ ] DIC
- [ ] Factor VII deficiency
> Cholestasis (here suspicious for biliary atresia) impairs absorption of fat-soluble vitamin K. Late VKDB can occur even after IM prophylaxis in cholestatic infants. Isolated ↑PT/INR with normal platelets/fibrinogen fits vitamin K deficiency; the direct bilirubin will be high.
:::

## Neonatal thrombosis {peds #neonatal-thrombosis refs=ash-peds-vte-2025}

- **> 80–90% of neonatal VTE is catheter-related** (UVC, UAC, CVC).
- **Renal vein thrombosis** is the most common non-catheter VTE in neonates. Triad: **flank mass, hematuria, thrombocytopenia.** Risks: IDM, dehydration, asphyxia, polycythemia.
- **Purpura fulminans in the first days of life:** rapidly spreading purpuric skin necrosis plus DIC. Think **homozygous protein C (or S) deficiency**. Give **FFP 10–20 mL/kg or protein C concentrate** immediately; check levels before replacement if possible.
- **ASH/ISTH 2025** [@ash-peds-vte-2025]:
  - Neonatal RVT: anticoagulation suggested. Non-life-threatening RVT: anticoagulation alone (no thrombolysis). Life-threatening (e.g., bilateral with renal failure): thrombolysis then anticoagulation.
  - Neonatal **occlusive** portal vein thrombosis: anticoagulation. **Non-occlusive:** no anticoagulation.
  - **DOACs are not recommended in preterm neonates.** Use LMWH or UFH.
- LMWH (enoxaparin) in neonates needs higher weight-based doses (~1.5–1.7 mg/kg q12h) with anti-Xa monitoring (target 0.5–1.0 U/mL at 4–6 h).

## Down syndrome: TAM and myeloid leukemia {peds #down-syndrome refs=who-haem5-myeloid}

- **Neonates with trisomy 21** often have polycythemia, thrombocytopenia, neutrophilia and macrocytosis. Get a **CBC with smear at birth.**
- **Transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM):**
  - Seen in ~10%. Circulating megakaryoblasts carry an acquired **GATA1** mutation.
  - Signs: leukocytosis, hepatomegaly (liver fibrosis), effusions, sometimes hydrops.
  - **Most resolve spontaneously by ~3 months.**
  - Severe TAM (hyperviscosity, liver failure, hydrops, DIC) → **low-dose cytarabine**.
- **~20% of TAM survivors develop myeloid leukemia of Down syndrome (ML-DS)** by age 4 (acute megakaryoblastic). ML-DS has **excellent outcomes with reduced-intensity chemotherapy**.
- **ALL risk is ~10–20× higher in DS.** These children have **more treatment toxicity** (infection, mucositis, methotrexate toxicity), so dose modifications are standard.

:::pearl
TAM blasts plus organomegaly in a Down syndrome newborn usually means **watch, don't treat**. But follow CBCs (e.g., every 3 months to age 4) for ML-DS.
:::
