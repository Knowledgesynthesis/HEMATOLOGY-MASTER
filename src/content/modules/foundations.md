## Hematopoiesis across development {both #hematopoiesis refs=nathan-oski}

| Stage | Main site | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 3–8 | Yolk sac (primitive erythropoiesis, embryonic Hb) | Embryonic Hb Gower/Portland |
| ~6 wk → birth | **Fetal liver** (peaks 2nd trimester) | Hepatosplenomegaly in fetal anemia (hydrops) |
| ~5th month → life | **Bone marrow** | Sole normal site after birth |
| Infancy → adulthood | All bones → axial skeleton (vertebrae, pelvis, sternum, proximal femur/humerus) | Iliac crest is the biopsy site from ~1 year; tibia in small infants |

- **Extramedullary hematopoiesis** after birth means marrow can't keep up (thalassemia major, myelofibrosis, severe chronic hemolysis): hepatosplenomegaly, paraspinal masses, "chipmunk" facies from marrow expansion.
- Regulators: **EPO** (kidney; rises within hours of hypoxia), **TPO** (liver; constitutive — level is set by platelet mass), **G-CSF** (neutrophils), IL-7 (lymphoid).
- Output: ~2 × 10¹¹ RBCs/day; RBC lifespan ~120 days (term neonate ~60–90 days, preterm ~35–50 days).

:::pearl
Short neonatal RBC survival plus falling EPO after birth explains the **physiologic nadir**. It is a normal developmental state, not a disease. See [Neonatal Hematology](/learn/neonatal#neonatal-anemia).
:::

## Reading the CBC like a hematologist {both #cbc refs=harriet-lane,nathan-oski}

Go in this order: **Is it real → which lines → size → production → smear.**

| Parameter | What it tells you | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Hb / Hct | Oxygen-carrying mass (a concentration) | Hemodilution and hemoconcentration; acute bleeding: Hb is normal at first |
| MCV | Average RBC size | Mixed populations (IDA + B12) can average to "normal"; check RDW |
| MCH / MCHC | Hb per cell / concentration | **↑ MCHC → spherocytes (HS, AIHA)**, cold agglutinin artifact, lipemia |
| RDW | Size variation (anisocytosis) | ↑ early in IDA; often normal in thalassemia trait (not reliable alone) |
| RBC count | Number of cells | **High RBC count with microcytosis → thalassemia trait** |
| WBC + differential | Use **absolute** counts, not % | Lymphocyte predominance is normal from ~1 week to ~4–6 years |
| Platelets / MPV | Count and size | **EDTA pseudothrombocytopenia** (clumps) — confirm on smear or citrate tube |

**Pediatric MCV rule of thumb:** lower limit of normal ≈ **70 + age (years)** fL until it reaches adult 80 fL (~age 10). A 2-year-old with MCV 73 is normal; an adult with 73 is not.

**Common artifacts**
- Cold agglutinins: falsely ↑MCV and ↑MCHC, ↓RBC count. Warm the sample.
- Severe hyperglycemia/hypernatremia: falsely ↑MCV.
- Very high WBC (leukemia): falsely ↑Hb, ↑MCV. Spun Hct is truer.
- Clumped platelets/giant platelets: falsely ↓platelets.

:::check
A well 2-year-old has Hb 11.8 g/dL, MCV 74 fL, RDW normal. What is the interpretation?
- [ ] Microcytic anemia; start iron
- [x] Normal for age
- [ ] Thalassemia trait until proven otherwise
- [ ] Spurious; repeat with citrate tube
> At 0.5–2 years the lower limit of Hb is ~10.5 g/dL and MCV ~70 fL (rule of thumb: 70 + age in years). Using adult cut-offs (Hb 12, MCV 80) mislabels normal toddlers as anemic.
:::

| 12–18 y, male | 14.5 (13.0) | 43 (37) | 88 (78) |

**WBC / neutrophils**
- Birth: WBC 9–30 × 10⁹/L, neutrophil-dominant; falls over the first week.
- 1 month to ~4 years: lymphocytes > neutrophils (normal ANC can be 1.0–1.5 in infants).
- Neutropenia: ANC < 1.5 × 10⁹/L from ~1 year (< 1.0 in infants 2–12 months).
- Platelets: 150–450 × 10⁹/L at all ages, including term neonates.

:::update WHO lowered the anemia cut-off for young children {year=2024}
The 2024 WHO guideline defines anemia as **Hb < 10.5 g/dL at 6–23 months** (was 11.0) and < 11.0 g/dL at 24–59 months [@who-hb-2024]. These are population thresholds. For an individual child, use age-specific laboratory ranges.
:::

## Reticulocytes: is the marrow trying? {both #retic refs=nathan-oski}

- **Absolute reticulocyte count (ARC)** = retic % × RBC count. Normal ~25–100 × 10⁹/L. In anemia, **> 100 × 10⁹/L** means the marrow is responding (hemolysis or blood loss); a low or normal ARC means under-production.
- **Corrected retic** = retic % × (patient Hct / normal Hct). Use the age-normal Hct in children.
- **Reticulocyte production index (RPI)** = corrected retic ÷ maturation time (Hct ≥ 40: 1.0 · 30–39: 1.5 · 20–29: 2.0 · < 20: 2.5). **RPI < 2 = hypoproliferative; > 3 = appropriate response.**
- Newborns: retic 3–7% in the first days, falling to < 1% by 1–2 weeks (the EPO switch-off).
- **Reticulocyte Hb content (CHr / Ret-He)** falls within days of iron-restricted erythropoiesis, earlier than Hb. It is useful in infants and in CKD.

:::pitfall
A "normal" retic % in a severely anemic patient is **inappropriately low**. Retic 2% with Hct 18% gives RPI ≈ 0.3, which is marrow failure, not hemolysis. Always correct the count.
:::

:::check
Hct 20%, retic 6%. What is the RPI (normal Hct 45%)?
- [ ] 6.0
- [ ] 2.7
- [x] ≈ 1.3
- [ ] 0.6
> Corrected retic = 6 × 20/45 ≈ 2.7%. Hct 20–29% → maturation factor 2.0 → RPI ≈ 1.3. That is less than 2, an **inadequate** response despite a retic % that looks high. Think concurrent iron/B12/folate deficiency, parvovirus, or marrow involvement.
:::

## Iron physiology and hepcidin {both #iron refs=who-ferritin-2020,aap-iron-2026}

- Absorption: duodenal enterocytes (DMT1 for Fe²⁺; heme iron separately). Export to plasma through **ferroportin**. Adults absorb ~1–2 mg/day, matching losses.
- **Hepcidin** (liver) degrades ferroportin and blocks absorption and macrophage release.
  - ↑ Inflammation (IL-6) → iron trapped in macrophages → **anemia of inflammation**.
  - ↓ Iron deficiency, hypoxia, ineffective erythropoiesis (erythroferrone). This explains **iron overload in non-transfused thalassemia**.
  - Inappropriately high hepcidin from TMPRSS6 mutations causes **IRIDA** (iron-refractory IDA), a pediatric cause of oral iron failure.
- **Fetal iron accretion happens mostly in the 3rd trimester.** Preterm birth, maternal IDA, maternal diabetes, growth restriction, and early cord clamping all reduce stores.
- Term stores last ~4–6 months. Rapid growth then drives demand: the RDA is **11 mg/day at 7–12 months**, the highest per kg of life.

| Test | Measures | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Ferritin | Stores | Acute-phase reactant: normal ferritin does not exclude ID if CRP is high |
| TSAT (Fe/TIBC) | Iron available to marrow | Diurnal and meal variation; < 16–20% = iron-restricted |
| TIBC / transferrin | ↑ in ID, ↓ in inflammation | ↑ in pregnancy and with OCPs |
| sTfR | Tissue iron need | Not affected by inflammation; ↑ with erythroid expansion |
| CHr / Ret-He | Iron to newly made RBCs | Early marker of deficiency |

## Hemoglobin types and switching {both #hb-switching refs=nathan-oski}

| Hb | Chains | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Gower 1/2, Portland | ζ/ε combinations | Embryo |
| **HbF** | α₂γ₂ | ~60–80% at term birth → **< 10% by 6 months** → adult level (< 1–2%) by 1–2 y |
| **HbA** | α₂β₂ | ~95–97% in adults |
| HbA₂ | α₂δ₂ | 2.0–3.5% in adults; **> 3.5% → β-thal trait** |
| Hb Barts | γ₄ | Newborns with α-thal (amount tracks gene deletions) |
| HbH | β₄ | HbH disease (3 α-gene deletions) |

- **α-chains are needed from fetal life on**, so severe α-thalassemia shows up **in utero** (hydrops).
- **β-chain disorders (SCD, β-thal) emerge at 6–24 months**, as HbF falls. Dactylitis in a 6–9-month-old is often the first SCD sign.
- HbF binds 2,3-BPG poorly, giving **higher O₂ affinity (P50 ~19–20 vs 26–27 mmHg)**. That favors placental O₂ transfer and is why HbF induction (hydroxyurea) helps SCD.

**O₂ dissociation curve.** Right shift (O₂ released more easily): ↑2,3-BPG, acidosis, fever, ↑CO₂. Left shift: HbF, CO, methemoglobin, alkalosis, hypothermia, high-affinity Hb variants. A left shift causes **erythrocytosis** with low P50, a cause of familial polycythemia.

## Hemostasis: the cell-based model {both #hemostasis refs=andrew-dev-hemostasis,wfh-2020}

1. **Primary hemostasis (platelet plug):** VWF bridges collagen to platelet **GPIb** (adhesion) → activation (ADP, TXA₂) → **GPIIb/IIIa** binds fibrinogen (aggregation). Defects cause **mucocutaneous** bleeding.
2. **Coagulation in vivo** starts with **tissue factor–FVIIa**, which generates a little thrombin. That thrombin amplifies through FVIII, FIX, FXI, FV on platelet surfaces, producing a thrombin burst and fibrin. Defects cause **deep tissue/joint** and delayed bleeding.
3. **FXIII** cross-links fibrin. **Deficiency does not prolong PT or aPTT.**
4. **Natural anticoagulants:** antithrombin, protein C/S (vitamin K–dependent), TFPI. **Fibrinolysis:** plasmin, regulated by α2-antiplasmin and PAI-1.

The PT/aPTT "extrinsic/intrinsic" split is a **test-tube construct**: PT covers VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen; aPTT covers XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, fibrinogen. FXII, prekallikrein and HMWK deficiencies prolong the aPTT but **do not cause bleeding**.

:::pearl Developmental hemostasis
At birth, vitamin K–dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X), contact factors, and protein C/S/antithrombin are **~30–60% of adult levels**. PT and aPTT are longer than adult ranges, but the system is **balanced** and healthy newborns don't bleed. Always interpret neonatal coagulation tests against **age- and gestation-specific ranges** [@andrew-dev-hemostasis].
:::

## Reading a peripheral smear {both #smear refs=nathan-oski}

Use the **monolayer** (where RBCs just touch) and scan the **feathered edge** for platelet clumps and large cells.

1. **RBCs:** size, color (hypochromia, polychromasia = retics), shape (spherocytes, schistocytes, targets, sickles, teardrops), inclusions (Howell-Jolly, basophilic stippling, Pappenheimer, parasites), nucleated RBCs.
2. **WBCs:** differential, blasts, left shift, toxic granulation, Döhle bodies, hypersegmentation (≥ 5% with 5 lobes or any with 6), atypical lymphocytes, smudge cells.
3. **Platelets:** ~1 platelet per 10–20 RBCs is normal. Each platelet per oil field ≈ 15–20 × 10⁹/L. Check for giant or tiny platelets and clumps.

The [Smear Atlas](/tools/smear-atlas) has illustrated findings and a flashcard drill.
